EX Refinement Levels

The plan for letting you set each hero's EX refinement level (R0–R4) next to their paragon level (P0–P4) — on both teams, saved automatically, carried in share links, and edited from a new control bar under each board. Reviewed in two audit rounds; every decision is settled and the design is ready to build.

1 · What we're building

Today the app tracks one upgrade per hero: paragon. This design adds a second one — EX refinement (R0 through R4) — for every hero on both teams. You'll see both upgrades in a small split pill under each portrait, edit it from a control bar under the board, and it will ride along in saved teams and share links just like paragon does.

Just as important: the storage format is being rebuilt so that the next upgrade type after refinement (and the ten after that) can be added without redoing any of this. Refinement is the second passenger, not a special case.

Your opponent's refinement is visible and editable too. We only track the level itself, not the exact stat boost it grants — whoever consumes the data can decide what a level is worth.

2 · How the data is saved & shared

The app stores your boards in the browser and packs them into the text of share links. Today, paragon has its own private slot in that format. The new design replaces it with one general-purpose upgrades list: each entry says which team, which hero, which kind of upgrade, what level. Paragon is upgrade kind #1, refinement is kind #2, and a future upgrade just takes the next number — the list itself never changes shape. Only non-zero levels are stored, so an untouched board adds nothing to a link.

Kind numbers are never reused or changed once assigned, and entries are always written in one fixed order — that ordering is what lets the app tell "this saved team differs from the board" by a simple byte-for-byte comparison, without false alarms.

There are two link formats, and they're handled differently:

  • Teams links and saved teams use a text-based format, where the upgrades list appears directly.
  • Arena links use an ultra-compact binary format. There, all upgrades — paragon included — share one new generic spot, added once. Paragon's old private spot is retired outright rather than kept around as a special case: no legacy format survives. During the converter month the app still reads the old spot; after that, an old Arena link that carried paragon simply stops loading (the board comes up empty) — accepted, since old links are expendable.

The format also reserves hero-number 0 for future team-wide upgrades — verified free in the code: real hero ids start at 1, the stand-in "placeholder" units use 9001–9007, companions use 10000 and up, and every existing validation rejects 0 today, so nothing has ever used it. When a team-wide upgrade actually ships, the writing side needs a small, known change; the format itself is ready.

Anything the app doesn't recognize in incoming data — an unknown upgrade kind, a garbled entry — is simply dropped. There's no scenario where a "newer version" of the app writes data an older one must protect: the app is a single hosted website, so once it updates, no old version is running anywhere. Unrecognized data can only be a hand-crafted or corrupted link, and discarding junk is the right answer.

The one-month converter

Existing saved data (your teams, your boards) uses the old paragon slot, so a temporary converter ships with the release, in one deletable module with step-by-step removal instructions — the project's standard pattern for migrations. It does two things:

  • On-the-fly conversion: any old-format data the app reads — a saved team, an old link someone opens, an imported file — is converted the moment it's read. Every read path funnels through a single choke point, so one small function covers them all.
  • A one-time cleanup: on your first visit after the release, everything saved in the browser — the saved-team library, all four modes' boards, and the Arena board — is rewritten in the new format, so nothing depends on which pages you happen to open during the month. The cleanup is careful by design: it only rewrites what it can fully read, it preserves the wrappers around stored data byte-for-byte, and it marks itself "done" only after every write succeeds — if anything fails (say, storage is full), it simply tries again next visit. Running twice is harmless.

Imported files are covered too — verified in code: importing an export file runs every team through the same validation-and-conversion path as everything else, so while the converter is live, an old export imports with its paragon levels intact and true duplicates are recognized and skipped.

About a month after release, the converter is deleted, per project policy: migration code is always temporary.

What breaks when the converter is deleted — accepted Old Teams share links posted before the release, and old export files sitting on disk, silently lose their paragon numbers when opened after the converter is gone (the teams themselves still load fine — only the levels reset to zero). Re-importing such an old export can also create a duplicate "(imported)" copy of a team you already have. Old Arena links that carried paragon stop loading entirely (empty board); paragon-less ones keep working. This is a deliberate trade: the app is a hosted website with no old versions in circulation, and stale links and exports are explicitly expendable.

3 · How the app keeps track while you play

Each board keeps a small ledger: for every team-and-hero pair, the hero's upgrade levels. Everything that happens to a hero — moving them, swapping two heroes, dragging one to another board, clearing a team, loading a saved team onto one side — already moves or clears their paragon through a handful of well-defined hand-off points. The design generalizes those hand-offs to carry the whole ledger entry instead of just the paragon number. The payoff: refinement (and every future upgrade) automatically behaves exactly like paragon in every drag, swap, and clear, with zero new bookkeeping — it's physically impossible for a hero to move and leave their refinement behind.

4 · What it looks like

The split pill

Both upgrades live in one slim split pill sitting on the bottom edge of each portrait — paragon on the left half, refinement on the right, separated by a slanted white seam. (The design started as two corner badges, but on 5v5 boards neighboring portraits sit so close that corner badges collided; a pill tucked under the portrait can never touch its neighbor.) The seam stays visible at every level, so an all-gray pill still clearly reads as two halves. The pill keeps the crisp white outline today's paragon badge has. It's a label, not a button — all editing happens in the control bar.

The colors tell a progress story on the refinement side: R0–R1 are quiet gray, R2–R3 warm up to a light red tint, and R4 turns full red with white text. Paragon stays simpler — gray until P4, which gets a silver-blue fill. White text appears only on the two maxed fills; on the silver-blue it's slightly below formal contrast guidelines, accepted because the pill is bold, tiny, and the fill alone already signals "maxed". When only one of the two layers is switched on in settings, the pill collapses to a one-color chip that keeps its pill shape (a raised minimum width stops it shrinking into a small circle).

The refinement half warms up in steps — gray, light tint from R2, full red at R4 — while the paragon half stays gray until P4. The white slanted seam is always visible, so every pill reads the same shape. Specimens render in the app's light palette by design.

The refinement layer gets its own on/off switch in the grid-info settings, nested under the hero-card option just like paragon's; switching a layer off hides its half of the pill. On the read-only share view, the pills show but nothing is editable, and visibility follows the viewer's own settings — same as paragon today. In Chinese the feature is called 精炼, the in-game term.

The control bar ("dock")

All editing moves out of the panel header and into a new floating bar under each board: a white card with rounded corners and a soft shadow, visually "popping" off the page. Every board gets its own bar, so the delete and bulk buttons always sit right next to the heroes they affect — on the Arena there's one board and one bar, exactly as mocked.

Reading the bar left to right: a trash button that clears the whole ally team (two taps — the first arms it with a pulsing ring, the second confirms), the ally bulk buttons (↺ reset to 0, ⌃ raise everyone to max, +1), the centered P / R selector, and the mirrored enemy side. The selector chips work like checkboxes: light up P to edit paragon, R to edit refinement, or both to edit both at once ("ALL"). The last lit chip can't be switched off, so there's always an active layer. The selection is shared across the whole page — flip a chip on any board's bar and every bar follows.

Editing rules, in plain terms

  • Tap a portrait with one layer selected: that hero's level steps up by one, wrapping from 4 back to 0 — exactly how paragon tapping works today. With ALL selected, a tap raises both levels by one, stopping at max — and once both are at max, the next tap wraps them together back to 0, just like the single-layer behavior. Mid-range values never wrap independently, so the two counters can't scramble each other.
  • Which layer is being edited is always visible: the lit chip plus a ring around the pill on every portrait. No colored button styling beyond that — the controls stay neutral.
  • The bar only appears when it makes sense: when the hero-card info layer is on and the page is editable — the same rule the hero panel follows today. That also keeps it out of the map editor, and avoids stacking a third trash icon under boards for people who never use these features. With both layer toggles off, the bar shows just the team-clear trashes.
  • No editing what you can't see: if a layer is switched off in settings (its half of the pill hidden), that layer can't be selected for editing — taps will never silently change an invisible number. And when the view is cropped to your own team, the enemy half of the bar hides; a side with no heroes has its buttons disabled. Deleting a team also cancels any in-progress tap-move gesture, so nothing is left pointing at a removed hero.

5 · The build plan

Three steps, each fully tested before the next:

  1. The plumbing — new storage format, the converter, and the generalized tracking. Completely invisible: paragon looks and behaves exactly as before. This lands as one pull request, because the format rename touches reading, writing, and display code all at once — there's no halfway point that both compiles and keeps data safe.
  2. The visuals — the split pill with its color ladder, the settings toggle, and the control bar on both pages.
  3. The companion notebook (the separate afkj-pvp project) learns to read the new upgrade list from shared links.

Each step is validated with the project's full lint, type-check, and test suite.

6 · What was double-checked

Claims the reviews verified against the actual code, including corrections to earlier drafts:

  1. Arena links use the binary format, not text; their legacy support is the temporary bit-1 reader deleted with the converter, and afterwards old paragon-bearing Arena links load an empty board (accepted). The Arena board saved in the browser is protected by the one-time cleanup.
  2. Every piece of Teams data — saved teams, links, imports, previews — really does flow through one decode function, so one conversion point covers everything.
  3. The hero hand-off surface is the 10-row table in §3, checked line by line.
  4. Today's badge sits upper-right with per-tier colors; the final scheme replaces it with the split pill and the gray → tint → red ladder (the per-tier fills are retired from the panel).
  5. Settings need no migration — new options simply default to off.
  6. Team thumbnails need no changes (they don't read upgrade data).
  7. No scenario was found where data gets corrupted — every failure mode is a clean, known loss, and each one is listed in §2's accepted-consequences note.
  8. The Arena page builds its layout differently than Teams, so its control bar needs its own mount (a round-2 catch — the first draft would have shipped the Arena with no controls).
  9. Saved boards live inside a small wrapper structure; the one-time cleanup preserves it byte-for-byte (a round-2 catch — a naive rewrite would have wiped boards in all four modes).

8 · Decisions

Owner-decided (from the design conversation)

  • Deployment model: the app is a single hosted deployment — "once it updates there won't be old versions available anywhere else." No design may assume version skew (old clients reading new data, rollbacks, parallel versions); the only transient exception is a stale browser tab open across a deploy.
  • Migration policy: converters/shims are TEMPORARY — one deletable module + removal runbook, deleted ~1 month after release; permanent conversion or translation fallbacks for legacy formats are out of policy (CLAUDE.md). Old links and old export files are explicitly expendable after removal.
  • Serialization: the generic u upgrade list — chosen over a paragon-style sibling key with the trade-offs re-examined under the temporary-converter policy; future extensibility is non-negotiable, and at 10+ upgrade types the sibling-key option costs per-attr boilerplate forever plus a binary format extension after two more attrs, versus one one-time converter. The generic bit-3 upgrades section (§2) follows from the same call, and paragon rides it too: the binary format's old paragon spot is deleted outright, not kept as a legacy special case — old paragon-bearing Arena links breaking after the converter's removal is accepted ("I don't care about links breaking"), and the freed-up spot becomes available for future use rather than being roped off forever.
  • ALL-mode tap wraps at max: with both layers selected, a tap raises both (stopping at max); once every selected layer is at max, the next tap wraps them all to 0 — matching the single-layer wrap.
  • Control bar: one per board — delete and bulk buttons stay adjacent to what they act on; the P/R selection is one page-global state shared by all bars. Visual style: the floating white "pop" card (rounded corners, soft shadow).
  • Pill palette (iterated live in the app): gray #cfc8bb/#4a463d for P0–P3 and R0–R1; R2–R3 light red tint #f5cdc2 so mid refinement shows as progress; P4 silver-blue #8fa7c8 white text (S3); R4 red #e4938a white text (lightened from the original #dd7a6c pick). The pill keeps a white border.
  • Split pill, not corner badges: corner badges collided between neighboring portraits on 5v5 boards, so both layers merged into one slanted split pill under each portrait (P left, R right, always-visible white seam). A lone visible layer keeps pill proportions via a raised minimum width instead of collapsing to a small circle. The pill is display-only (too small to be a reliable tap target).
  • Selector: independent P/R toggle chips (both lit = ALL).
  • Control colors: neutral — no per-layer tinting; the "what am I editing" signal is the filled selector chip plus pill rings only.
  • Locale: zh 精炼 (the in-game term).
